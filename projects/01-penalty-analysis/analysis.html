Should the Fouled Player Take the Penalty Himself?

A player gets fouled by the goalkeeper in the box. He picks himself up, dusts off, and walks to the spot. Should he? Theory says no: physically jarred, emotionally agitated, while the keeper across from him has every reason to be locked in. A one-tailed z-test across the top 5 European leagues asks whether the data backs the case for handing the ball to a designated specialist.

Overview

It happens often: a player gets fouled in the box by the goalkeeper, picks himself up, and walks straight to the spot. The question for the bench is whether that's a good idea. Theory says no: the just-fouled player is physically jarred and emotionally agitated, while the keeper across from him has just committed a foul that elevated his afternoon into a high-leverage moment and is locked in. The taker should hand the ball to a designated specialist.

The test isolates exactly that scenario (GK caused the foul AND the fouled player took the penalty himself) and compares the conversion rate against every other penalty in scope. Because the theory predicts conversion drops in this specific situation, the test is one-tailed.

Data & Method

Sources: Per-match player-level statistics for the top 5 European leagues (FBref-derived; consolidated into FULL_DICT_ROWS).

Competitions: Premier League, La Liga, Serie A, Bundesliga, Ligue 1.

Sample: [loading]

Unit of analysis: one penalty. [loading]

Treatment: linked penalties where the goalkeeper conceded the foul (PKcon=1) and the player taking the penalty (PKatt=1) was also the player who won it (PKwon=1).

Control: every other linked penalty: the GK conceded but someone else took it, an outfielder conceded (any taker), or no foul was recorded (e.g. handball).

Test: one-tailed two-proportion z-test (statsmodels.proportions_ztest(alternative='smaller')) with α = 0.05, plus Wilson 95% CIs on each proportion and a Newcombe 95% CI on the difference.

Checks: three alternative control groups, two sensitivity samples, a logistic regression that accounts for who takes the kick, an equivalence test and a Bayesian version of the comparison, each explained in plain words below, in full in the methodology.

Primary Test · One-Tailed

Should he take it himself?

H₀: conversion_treatment = conversion_control
H₁: conversion_treatment < conversion_control  (one-tailed, lower)
Why one-tailed: the theory predicts a directional drop (hurt + agitated taker, motivated GK). A two-tailed test would dilute power against an asymmetric prediction.
